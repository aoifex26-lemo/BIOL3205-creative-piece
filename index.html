Meet the Patient

PATIENT PROFILE

Your health journey

You are a 22-year-old university student studying music. Your health journey has included gestational diabetes in both pregnancies, persistently elevated fasting blood glucose levels, and a diagnosis of Hashimoto’s disease three years ago. You have also been diagnosed with atypical MODY.

FAMILY HISTORY

A shared pattern in your family

All three of your siblings have high fasting blood sugar and borderline HbA1c results. **HbA1c is a blood test that shows your average blood sugar level over the past two to three months.** Despite having different lifestyles, your siblings show a similar pattern. Further family studies may help us understand why and whether it runs in the family.

Understanding Your Genetic “Score”

Click to look at the explanation of your genetic testing result

Your genetic test found a change in one of your two copies of the GCK gene, which provides instructions for making glucokinase, an enzyme that helps your pancreas sense blood sugar and regulate insulin release (Delvecchio et al., 2020). The change, called c.208+3A>G, is located in an intron, a section normally removed during RNA processing. Computer predictions suggest it may affect RNA splicing, which helps prepare RNA instructions for making a protein, but this has not been confirmed experimentally (Haliloglu et al., 2016). The notation p.(?) means that the effect on the protein is unknown. Your result is classified as a variant of uncertain significance (VUS), so it does not yet prove that this genetic change causes your elevated blood sugar or your atypical MODY diagnosis.

What Is MODY?

Like different musical compositions, different types of MODY can have different characteristics and clinical effects.

Click to read more about MODY

MODY stands for Maturity-Onset Diabetes of the Young. It is a group of uncommon forms of diabetes caused by disease-related changes in a single gene that affect how the body regulates blood glucose (Shields et al., 2012).

Unlike type 1 diabetes, which is usually caused by an autoimmune attack on insulin-producing cells, or type 2 diabetes, which often involves insulin resistance, MODY results from changes in a single gene (Roglic, 2016; Delvecchio et al., 2020).

Understanding which type of MODY a person has can help the healthcare team choose the most appropriate treatment and monitoring plan.

What Does This Mean for Your Health?

BLOOD GLUCOSE

Long-term blood glucose risks

Persistently elevated blood glucose may increase the risk of long-term complications, depending on the degree of hyperglycaemia and the underlying condition. Confirmed GCK-MODY is generally associated with mild, stable hyperglycaemia and a low risk of diabetes-related complications (Delvecchio et al., 2020). However, because your GCK variant is currently classified as a VUS, your specific long-term risks cannot be determined from this result alone.

PREGNANCY

Pregnancy-related risks

If you plan another pregnancy, early consultation with your healthcare team will be important. In confirmed GCK-MODY, pregnancy management may depend on both the mother’s and the fetus’s GCK genotype, as the fetal genotype can influence fetal growth. Your management plan should be individualised, particularly because your genetic result is not yet conclusive (Delvecchio et al., 2020).

THYROID

Thyroid health

You also have Hashimoto’s disease, an autoimmune condition affecting the thyroid gland, which requires appropriate follow-up. Some research has reported an increased prevalence of autoimmune thyroid disease in patients with GCK-MODY, although further evidence is needed to clarify this association (Calcaterra et al., 2019).

What Can We Do Next?

Just as musicians practise, adjust their performance, and work with others to refine their music, regular monitoring, personalised care, and family genetic testing can help us better understand your health and guide the next steps.

01 / MONITORING

Monitoring

Regular fasting blood glucose and HbA1c testing can help assess glucose control. An oral glucose tolerance test (OGTT) may also be considered when further assessment of glucose tolerance is needed, depending on your clinical situation (Hulín et al., 2020).

02 / MANAGEMENT

Personalised Management

Patients with confirmed GCK-MODY typically have stable, mildly elevated fasting blood glucose levels and generally do not require glucose-lowering treatment (Hattersley & Patel, 2017). Maintaining a regular daily routine, eating a balanced diet, engaging in regular physical activity, and getting adequate sleep can support overall health.

03 / GENETIC FOLLOW-UP

Family Genetic Testing

Your three siblings also have elevated fasting blood glucose levels. Genetic counselling and family testing may help determine whether the GCK variant is associated with your family’s blood glucose patterns and whether additional genetic factors may contribute to differences between family members (Maltoni et al., 2015).

KEY TAKEAWAY

Your result is a clue, not a final answer

Your clinical history and family pattern deserve careful follow-up. The GCK result may be relevant, but the current evidence does not prove that c.208+3A>G causes your elevated blood glucose. Your GCK variant is currently classified as a variant of uncertain significance (VUS). Your healthcare team can combine genetic information with clinical findings and family studies to guide next steps.
